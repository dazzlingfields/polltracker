What if the polls miss again?

Each row adds one election’s complete error pattern to today’s polling average. Party errors remain linked, so gains and losses are not independently mixed.

2011 and 2014 predate Opportunity: its share is held fixed in those rows before any clipping and normalisation. Its historical performance at roughly 1–3% may not transfer to a 2026 threshold contest. Test it separately below.

Assumptions you can change

Manual swings are percentage points added before normalising to 100%. Try Opportunity −1 pp to test the threshold. Electorate floors are scenario assumptions, not electorate forecasts.

Defaults give ACT and TPM one electorate each, with zero floors for the other parties. Set actual expected wins when testing overhang. An independent seat reduces the 120-seat party allocation pool by one. “Other” is excluded from list allocation even when its combined share exceeds 5%, because it represents multiple parties. A minor party inside Other winning list seats is outside this model.

How accurate were the final polls?

Comparison: the last available poll from each of three long-running series before each election, across National, Labour, Green, ACT, NZ First and Māori / Te Pāti Māori. That is 15 polls, 90 party-poll comparisons and only five election cycles.

MAE is mean absolute error in percentage points. It is not “percentage accuracy”. Six common parties keep the comparison consistent across years. Opportunity is included separately where it existed, but excluded from headline accuracy scores. Poll fieldwork periods differ.

Error by polling series

Five polls per series. These are descriptive historical results, not current quality ratings. The 1News series changed methodology and name over this period; the commissioning outlet for Reid Research also changed. Other firms are excluded for a consistent five-election panel, not because they are judged inferior.

Average error by party

Signed error = result − three-poll average. Positive means polls understated the eventual result. Negative means polls overstated it. A persistent historical pattern is not guaranteed to repeat.

Does a bias correction improve the model?

Test the unadjusted average against adding half or all of the mean error from the training elections. Each test predicts vote shares, then clips negatives and normalises to 100% using the same procedure as the calculator. Accuracy is scored on the same six parties.

Leave-one-election-out holds out an entire election, so polls from the same election never appear in both training and evaluation. It is a retrospective diagnostic, since later years may train earlier held-out years. The forward-only test predicts 2014, 2017, 2020 and 2023 using only earlier elections. Neither is a validation of the 2026 poll roster or a probabilistic forecast. The baseline remains unadjusted because these broad bias corrections did not improve either test.

Method, demographics and limitations

Start with official 2023 final party-vote counts for every electorate, including TOP separately. Preserve each electorate’s relative party preferences using multiplicative national swing. Iteratively adjust party columns to the current national scenario and normalise electorate rows to 100%, weighting by 2023 valid party votes. This raking procedure reproduces the national target without negative local shares. General and Māori rolls enter together, without counting any historical vote twice.

Historical results retain the combined imprint of local demographics and political history; demographic effects are not separately estimated. This version assumes stable geographic voting odds and turnout. It has no candidate, incumbency, tactical voting, migration or age-specific swing model. Large changes for small parties, including Opportunity, are extrapolations.

Demographic model specification: before introducing demographic coefficients, reaggregate historical voting-place results to the 2025 boundaries and join matched Census age, education, deprivation, housing tenure and urban/rural composition. Fit regularised party log-ratios with historical support as an offset, validate on held-out elections and geographically held-out electorates, and retain demographic effects only if they improve prediction beyond this baseline. Rake the resulting estimates to the national polling target. Aggregate correlations must not be interpreted as individual voting behaviour. This work is pending; no invented demographic coefficients or win probabilities are applied here.

The current output is a conditional geographic scenario, not a calibrated election-day forecast. Tile colours indicate the largest party-vote share and do not determine MMP electorate exemptions or change the Parliament model’s assumed electorate wins.

A small, transparent model

  1. Use one recent poll per house. Filter enabled rows by fieldwork end date and publication date where verified. This is not a point-in-time archive of what was publicly available on every earlier date. Within the editable age window, select the newest row for each pollster key. Give each house weight 1, with Roy Morgan weight 0.5 by default (adjustable from 0 to 1). Normalise weights across eligible houses. Sample sizes are recorded but do not set weights.
  2. Retain the reported seven party shares. Other is 100 minus their sum. Reject negative shares and totals above 100. This preserves source rounding for individual parties without claiming the residual is independently measured.
  3. Calibrate on five election averages. Use final 1News, Reid Research and Roy Morgan polls for 2011, 2014, 2017, 2020 and 2023. The actual party votes come from official final results, not election-night counts. Historical polling figures are transcribed from the linked polling tables, with selected original reports checked.
  4. Keep the centre unadjusted by default. Optional historical correction affects the six consistently observed parties. Other balances that shift. Opportunity gets no fitted correction because it has only three election observations.
  5. Replay joint errors. Add one historical result-minus-average vector to the current polling average. Clip negative categories and normalise to 100%. The five scenarios always use the original full historical errors, regardless of the optional correction selected for the centre, to avoid applying the historical mean twice.
  6. Allocate seats using Sainte-Laguë. Eligible parties have at least 5% of the party vote or at least one assumed electorate win. Rank vote-share quotients with divisors 1, 3, 5, 7… for 120 minus independent seats, then apply electorate floors. Overhang increases Parliament’s size; the majority is floor(total seats / 2) + 1.
error[p,y] = final_result[p,y] − mean(final_polls[p,y])
MAE = mean(abs(error))
baseline[p] = sum(house_weight × latest_eligible_poll[p]) / sum(house_weight)
scenario[p] = normalise(max(0, baseline[p] + historical_error[p,y] + manual_swing[p]))

What this model can and cannot tell you

It shows the scale and direction of past misses, current support under a reproducible averaging rule, and the sensitivity of seats to vote shares, thresholds and electorate assumptions. It does not separate sampling error, turnout error, house effects and changes in voter preference after fieldwork. All are mixed in the observed poll-to-result error.

It is a polling snapshot with historical stress tests, not a calibrated election-day forecast. Five election-level observations are too few for precise probabilities or a dependable 95% interval. Current polls use a different mix of firms from the historical panel. No economic variables, turnout model, campaign trend, electorate polling or coalition-negotiation model is fitted. You can enter additional campaign swings, but these are your assumptions, not estimated uncertainty.

2017 and 2020 official tables display one-decimal percentages; the stored two-decimal shares are consistent with official vote counts. 2023 uses the current corrected official table: National 38.08, Labour 26.91, Green 11.60, ACT 8.64, NZ First 6.08, TPM 3.08 and TOP 2.22. Older announcements and secondary tables have small differences. Seats here use an ordinary 120-seat election, not the special 2023 Port Waikato by-election sequence. Exact quotient ties are resolved by party order for determinism; an official tie would require the statutory procedure. List exhaustion and vacant seats are not modelled.
